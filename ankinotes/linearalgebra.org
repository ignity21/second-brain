*** Front
How do you state "vectors $\bm{u}$, $\bm{v}$, $\bm{w}$ are linearly independent" with one equation that treats all three vectors equally?

*** Back
$a\bm{u}+b\bm{v}+c\bm{w}=\bm{0}$ has only the solution $a=b=c=0$. If any other solution exists, one vector lies in the span of the others, so the set is dependent.

*** Front
A linear transformation $A$ squashes 3D space onto a plane. What are its rank and its column space, and what does its null space contain?

*** Back
Rank 2: the column space (all possible outputs $A\bm{v}$) is that plane. The null space contains every vector that lands on the origin, here a whole line of vectors, not just $\bm{0}$.

** Meaning of the determinant
:PROPERTIES:
:ANKI_NOTE_TYPE: CC Basic

*** Front
What does each of these say about a 2D transformation $M$: $det(M)=5$, $det(M)=-2$, $det(M)=0$?

*** Back
- $5$: areas grow by a factor of 5.
- $-2$: areas double and space is flipped over (orientation reverses).
- $0$: space is squashed into a lower dimension; the columns are linearly dependent and $M$ has no inverse.

*** Front
In theory a square matrix is invertible when $det(M) \ne 0$. Why is ~LA.det(M) != 0~ a poor test in NumPy, and what do you check instead?

*** Back
A nearly singular matrix has a tiny but non-zero determinant in floating point, so the test passes and the inverse is garbage. Compare ~LA.matrix_rank(M)~ with $n$, or check that ~LA.cond(M)~ is not huge. A truly singular matrix makes ~LA.inv~ raise ~LinAlgError: Singular matrix~.

*** Front
Write $(QR)^{-1}$ in terms of $Q^{-1}$ and $R^{-1}$, and explain the order.

*** Back
$(QR)^{-1}=R^{-1}Q^{-1}$. $QR$ applies $R$ first, then $Q$; undoing it must undo $Q$ first, then $R$, like taking off socks and shoes in reverse order.

*** Front
You want to squeeze points ~P~ with ~F_squeeze~ and then shear them with ~F_shear~, for many point sets. What single matrix do you precompute, and why does precomputing it help?

*** Back
~F_shear @ F_squeeze~: the transformation applied last goes on the left. Matrix multiplication is associative, so ~F_shear @ (F_squeeze @ P)~ equals ~(F_shear @ F_squeeze) @ P~, and multiplying the small matrices once avoids transforming every point twice.

*** Front
Why must $det(M-\lambda I)=0$ for $\lambda$ to be an eigenvalue of $M$?

*** Back
$M\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}$ rearranges to $(M-\lambda I)\bm{v}=\bm{0}$. An eigenvector is non-zero, so $M-\lambda I$ must send a non-zero vector to the origin, which means it squashes space to a lower dimension: its determinant is 0.

Source: Eigenvectors

*** Front
Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $M=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$.

*** Back
$det(M-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)(2-\lambda)=0$, so $\lambda=3$ and $\lambda=2$ (for a triangular matrix, the diagonal entries).
- $\lambda=3$: $\bm{v}=(1, 0)$.
- $\lambda=2$: $\bm{v}=(1, -1)$.

*** Front
How do eigenvectors make $M^{10}$ cheap to compute?

*** Back
With eigenvectors as the columns of $P$ and eigenvalues on the diagonal of $D$, $M=PDP^{-1}$. In $M^{10}$ the inner $P^{-1}P$ pairs cancel, leaving $M^{10}=PD^{10}P^{-1}$, and $D^{10}$ just raises each diagonal entry to the 10th power.

*** Front
The columns of $B$ are the new basis vectors written in the old coordinates. Which matrix converts a vector's new coordinates to old coordinates, which converts the other way, and what is the shortcut when $B$ is orthonormal?

*** Back
- New → old: $B\bm{x}_{new}=\bm{x}_{old}$.
- Old → new: $B^{-1}\bm{x}_{old}=\bm{x}_{new}$.
- Orthonormal $B$: $B^{-1}=B^T$, so no inversion is needed.

*** Front
What is the geometric form of $\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}$, and what does $\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}=0$ tell you?

*** Back
$\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}=\|\bm{x}\|\|\bm{y}\|\cos\theta$. A zero dot product (with non-zero vectors) means $\cos\theta=0$: the vectors are perpendicular.

*** Front
Computing the angle between vectors with ~np.arccos(x @ y / LA.norm(x) / LA.norm(y))~ occasionally returns ~nan~. Why, and what is the fix?

*** Back
Floating-point error can push the cosine slightly outside $[-1, 1]$ (for example ~1.0000000002~ for parallel vectors), where arccos is undefined. Clip first: ~np.arccos(np.clip(cos_theta, -1, 1))~.

*** Front
In Gram–Schmidt, $e_1$ is already a unit vector. How do you get $e_2$ from $v_2$?

*** Back
Remove the part of $v_2$ along $e_1$, then normalize: $u_2=v_2-(v_2\cdot e_1)e_1$, $e_2=\frac{u_2}{\|u_2\|}$. For $e_3$, subtract the projections onto both $e_1$ and $e_2$.

*** Front
What does the singular value decomposition $M=U\Sigma V^T$ of any $m\times n$ matrix say geometrically?

*** Back
Every linear transformation is a rotation ($V^T$, $n\times n$ orthogonal), then a scaling and projection along the axes ($\Sigma$, $m\times n$ diagonal, holding the singular values), then another rotation ($U$, $m\times m$ orthogonal).

** Pseudoinverse via SVD

*** Front
How is the pseudoinverse $X^{+}$ computed from the SVD $X=U\Sigma V^T$, and why is it used instead of the inverse in linear regression?

*** Back
$X^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}U^T$, where $\Sigma^{+}$ sets singular values below a tiny threshold to zero, inverts the rest, and is transposed. It exists for any matrix, including non-square data matrices and ones with dependent columns, where $X^TX$ has no inverse.

Source: Pseudoinverse

*** Front
Find the Jacobian matrix of $u(x,y)=x-2y$, $v(x,y)=3y-2x$. Why does it not depend on $x$ and $y$?

*** Back
$J=\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 \\ -2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$ (rows: $u$, $v$; columns: $\partial x$, $\partial y$). The functions are linear, so their gradients are the same everywhere; the Jacobian of a linear map is the map's own matrix.

*** Front

*** Back
$\frac{df}{dt}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \vec{x}}\frac{\partial \vec{x}}{\partial \vec{u}}\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}$, with shapes $(1\times n)(n\times m)(m\times 1)$. Backpropagation evaluates this product from the left, one layer at a time.

** Second derivative test with the Hessian
:PROPERTIES:
:ANKI_NOTE_TYPE: CC Basic

*** Front
At a critical point the gradient is zero. How does the Hessian tell a minimum, a maximum and a saddle point apart in general, and what is the two-variable shortcut?

*** Back
By the signs of its eigenvalues: all positive → minimum; all negative → maximum; mixed → saddle point; some zero → inconclusive.

Two variables only: $det(H)>0$ and $H_{11}>0$ → minimum; $det(H)>0$ and $H_{11}<0$ → maximum; $det(H)<0$ → saddle. The shortcut fails in 3D: a Hessian with $det(H)>0$ and $H_{11}>0$ can still have mixed eigenvalues.

Source: The Hessian

*** Front
What direction does $\bm{a}\times\bm{b}$ point in for two linearly independent 3D vectors, and what happens when they are parallel?

*** Back
Perpendicular to both $\bm{a}$ and $\bm{b}$, oriented by the right-hand rule (fingers from $\bm{a}$ toward $\bm{b}$, thumb along $\bm{a}\times\bm{b}$). Parallel vectors give the zero vector.
