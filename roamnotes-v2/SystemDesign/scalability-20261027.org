#+title: Scalability in System Design
#+filetags: :SystemDesign:

* Intuition
Scalability is not a yes/no property. The useful question is: if load grows in a particular way, what are the options for coping with it? Answering that needs two descriptions: of the load, and of the performance.

* Describing Load
Load is described by a few *load parameters*, chosen for the system's architecture:
- requests per second, the ratio of reads to writes, cache hit rate, simultaneously active users;
- Twitter's home timeline: the distribution of followers per user, which determines the fan-out (how many timelines one tweet must be written to). Precomputing timelines on write is cheap for most users but expensive for celebrities with millions of followers, so a hybrid was used: fan out on write for most users, merge celebrities' tweets on read.

* Describing Performance
Two ways to look at it:
1. Increase a load parameter and keep the resources fixed: how does performance change?
2. Increase a load parameter: how much must the resources grow to keep performance the same?

| Metric | Meaning | Matters for |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput | Records processed per second, or time to run a job over a dataset | Batch processing |
| Response time | What the client sees: from sending the request to receiving the response | Online systems |
| Latency | The time a request waits to be handled, a part of response time | Diagnosing queueing |

- *Use percentiles, not the mean*: the median (p50) says what a typical request sees; p99 and p999 show the tail. Outliers come from context switches, lost packets and retransmission, garbage collection pauses, and page faults that read from disk.
- Amazon sets internal targets at p999, because the slowest requests often belong to the customers with the most data, who are the most valuable.
- *Tail latency amplification*: a request that fans out to many backends is as slow as the slowest one. With 100 backends, even a p99 that is rarely hit on one backend is hit by most requests.
- Optimizing the very high percentiles is hard: they are dominated by random events outside your control, and the returns diminish.

* Coping with Load
- *Scale up* (a bigger machine) is simpler; *scale out* (more machines) is needed beyond one machine's limits or for availability. Stateless services scale out easily; stateful ones, especially databases, are hard.
- Keep the database on a single node until scaling cost or availability requirements force distribution.
- An architecture that scales well is built around assumptions about which operations are common and which are rare, the load parameters. The wrong assumptions make the scaling effort wasted or counterproductive.
