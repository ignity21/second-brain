#+title: Position Sizing and Risk per Trade
#+filetags: :Trading:

* Why Size by Risk
Losing streaks are not rare. Chance of at least one run of 10 losses in a row:

| Win rate | 100 trades | 500 trades | 1000 trades |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 4% | 22% | 39% |
| 40% | 21% | 71% | 92% |

Risking a fixed fraction $f$ of the account per trade, 10 straight losses leave $(1-f)^{10}$ of it:

| Risk per trade | Drawdown after 10 losses | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|---|
| 1% | 9.6% | 10.6% |
| 2% | 18.3% | 22.4% |
| 5% | 40.1% | 67.0% |
| 10% | 65.1% | 186.8% |

Recovering takes a larger gain than the loss, and the gap widens fast. Hence the rule of thumb: risk *2% per trade*, never more than *5%*.

* Position Size Formula
$$\text{Shares} = \frac{\text{account} \times \text{risk per trade}}{\text{entry price} - \text{stop price}}$$

Example: account $100,000, risk 2%, entry 4.56, stop 4.08.
- Max loss per trade: $100,000 × 2% = $2,000
- Risk per share: 4.56 − 4.08 = 0.48
- Shares: 2,000 / 0.48 = 4,166 (rounded down); loss if stopped: 4,166 × 0.48 = $1,999.68

The position is worth 4,166 × 4.56 ≈ $19,000, about 19% of the account. *Risk* is 2%, not the position. A tight stop gives a large position, so also cap the position value (buying power, concentration).

* Stops
- Decide the stop before entering. It goes where the trade idea is proven wrong, such as below support, the pattern's low, or a multiple of ATR (see [[id:ede0aad7-a06d-4c84-9081-1c8f40f122da][Technical Analysis]]), not at the loss you can stomach. The position size then follows from the stop, not the other way round.
- *Taking the loss is the important part.* Loss aversion makes a loss feel worse than an equal gain feels good, so traders move stops further away or remove them. That turns a 2% loss into an unbounded one.
- A stop order is not a guaranteed price: a gap through the stop fills at the next available price, so the real loss can exceed the plan. Size more conservatively ahead of earnings or overnight holds.

* Across Positions
Per-trade risk adds up. Five open positions at 2% each put 10% at risk. If they are correlated (same sector, same factor) they behave like one large position, so cap total open risk as well as risk per trade.
