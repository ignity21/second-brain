* Intuition
A share is a claim on a business. Fundamental analysis asks what the business earns, owns, and generates in cash, then compares that with the price. Every valuation ratio has the form /price ÷ something the company produces or owns/: the lower it is, the less you pay per unit of that thing. Whether a lower number is a bargain or a warning depends on growth and quality, which is why ratios are compared, never read alone.

* How the Three Statements Connect
| Statement | Shows | Identity |
|---|---|---|
| Income statement | Profit over a period, on an accrual basis | Net income (earnings) = revenue − expenses |
| Balance sheet | What the company owns and owes at a moment | Assets = liabilities + equity |
| Cash flow statement | Cash that actually came in and went out over a period | Operating + investing + financing cash flows |

The link that matters: net income not paid out as dividends stays in the company as retained earnings, so

$$\text{Equity}_{t} = \text{Equity}_{t-1} + \text{Net income} - \text{Dividends}$$

Book value is equity: total assets − total liabilities. /Tangible/ book value also subtracts intangible assets such as goodwill, which could not be sold separately.

* Ratio Map
| Group | Ratio | Formula | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valuation | P/E | price / EPS, EPS = net income / shares | How many years of current earnings the price buys |
| Valuation | Forward P/E | price / next year's estimated EPS | Same, on expected earnings |
| Valuation | P/B | price / book value per share | Price relative to net assets; meaningful for banks and asset-heavy firms |
| Valuation | FCF yield | FCF per share / price | Cash return on the price; higher = cheaper |
| Profitability | ROE | net income / equity (start-of-period or average) | Profit per dollar of shareholders' money |
| Profitability | FCF margin | FCF / revenue | Cash generated per dollar of sales |
| Solvency | Debt-to-equity | total debt / shareholders' equity | Ability to carry long-term debt |
| Activity | Asset turnover | revenue / total assets | How hard the assets work |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | current assets / current liabilities | Ability to pay bills due within a year |

Size by market capitalization (price × shares outstanding): small cap 300M–2B, mid cap 2B–10B, large cap above 10B (USD).

** Ownership and Short Interest
| Metric | Meaning | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Insider ownership and transactions | Shares held and traded by executives and directors | Insider buying is a stronger signal than selling; insiders sell for many reasons |
| Institutional ownership | Share of float held by funds | High → more stable, mean-reverting behavior (see [[id:fac02460-82e1-4da3-9ea7-702c25095669][Trading Strategy Types]]) |
| Short float | Shares sold short / float | How many investors bet on a fall |
| Short ratio (days to cover) | Short interest / average daily volume | High → shorts would need many days to buy back, so a rally can turn into a short squeeze |

* Free Cash Flow
$$\text{FCF} = \text{operating cash flow} - \text{capital expenditure (CAPEX)}$$

- Operating cash flow is the cash produced by normal business operations; CAPEX is money spent to maintain or buy physical assets.
- Why look past earnings: earnings follow accounting choices (revenue recognition, depreciation); FCF is money that actually entered and left. "Earnings are an opinion, cash is a fact."
- FCF conversion = FCF / net income. Persistent conversion well below 100% means reported profit is not turning into cash. A common screen asks for at least 85%.
- What a company can do with FCF: reinvest for growth, pay down debt, acquire other companies, pay dividends, buy back shares. How it chooses tells you how management sees its own growth opportunities.

* Worked Example
Two companies IPO at $1.5 per share with no intangible assets. A has 30M shares and $2M liabilities; B has 90M shares and $5M liabilities. Year 1: A earns $2M and pays all of it out; B earns $5M and pays out $1M.

| Year 1 | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| Equity | 45M + 2M − 2M = $45M | 135M + 5M − 1M = $139M |
| Assets | $47M | $144M |
| Market price | $2.00 | $2.50 |
| Market cap | $60M | $225M |
| Book value per share | $1.50 | $1.544 |
| P/B | 1.33 | 1.62 |
| EPS | $0.0667 | $0.0556 |
| P/E | 30 | 45 |
| ROE (on start equity) | 4.44% | 3.70% |
| Dividend per share (yield) | $0.0667 (3.33%) | $0.0111 (0.44%) |

B is less profitable per dollar of equity, yet the market pays 45× earnings for it versus 30× for A. The difference is what happens next: A pays out everything, so its book does not grow; B keeps 80% of its earnings and grows equity. A higher multiple is a bet on growth, and only pays off if the retained earnings later earn a good return.

* Dividend Discount Model
Value a stock as the present value of all future dividends. Assume the next dividend is $D_1$, dividends grow at a constant rate $g$, and the required return is $r$:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{1+r} + \frac{D_1(1+g)}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{D_1(1+g)^2}{(1+r)^3} + \cdots$$

This is a geometric series with first term $\frac{D_1}{1+r}$ and ratio $q = \frac{1+g}{1+r}$. It converges only when $q < 1$, i.e. $g < r$:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1/(1+r)}{1 - \frac{1+g}{1+r}} = \frac{D_1}{r-g}, \qquad D_1 = D_0(1+g)$$

$P_0$ is the stock's value today, $D_0$ the dividend just paid.

- *Sensitivity*: the value depends on $r - g$. With $D_1 = 1$, $r = 8\%$: $g = 5\% \Rightarrow P_0 = 33$; $g = 6\% \Rightarrow P_0 = 50$. One percentage point of assumed growth changes the value by half, so the output is only as good as the growth guess.
- *Assumptions*: constant growth forever, $g < r$, and dividends that reflect earnings. It does not fit companies that pay no dividends or grow fast for a few years; those need a multi-stage model or a cash-flow model.
- Lower interest rates lower $r$ and raise $P_0$, which is the link from [[id:916f4d11-e91d-4574-bfa2-befca1493e58][monetary policy]] to stock prices.

* Using the Ratios
- *Benchmark* against peers in the same industry: a P/E of 30 is expensive for a utility and cheap for a fast-growing software company.
- *Trend*: compare the same company across years; a ratio moving in the wrong direction matters more than its level.
